# Thoughts on "Compilers 2.0: AI as a Stochastic Optimizer"

The Twitter post [Compilers 2.0: AI as stochastic optimizer](https://x.com/cdleary/status/2094878051238887834?s=46) by Chris Leary proposed a new system. Below are my thoughts. 

I used LLM tools to get a better understanding of the article as well as to structure my response. I've broken this down into two parts: Part 1 contains my initial thoughts, and Part 2 uses an AI tool to frame those thoughts more effectively. 

> **TL;DR:** The viability of Leary’s stochastic AI optimizer ultimately depends on how precise, complete, and trustworthy the compiler’s correctness specification and verification mechanism are. Even if the AI-generated program is proven correct and faster, the engineering question remains whether humans can realistically diagnose, debug, and maintain such generated code when something goes wrong.

## part-2 AI polished version:

In the past, I have come across work such as SRTuner: Effective Compiler Optimization Customization by Exposing Synergistic Relations, Register-Pressure-Aware Instruction Scheduling Using Ant Colony Optimization, and projects that use genetic or other search-based techniques for program transformation and optimization. What I found interesting in these approaches is that they do not necessarily try to prove that they have found the globally optimal solution; instead, they search for a sufficiently good solution under a practical optimization budget. I am generally supportive of this style of compiler optimization. Chris Leary’s proposal seems to extend the same basic idea much further: given a high-level specification, the AI proposes a candidate implementation, the system checks whether it preserves the required semantics, benchmarks its performance, feeds that result back into the optimization process, and repeats this loop many times. What makes Leary’s proposal particularly compelling to me is this sustained iterative search—for example, allowing the optimizer to continue improving a kernel over roughly a 48-hour window rather than stopping after one optimization decision or one search phase. In principle, this gives the system more opportunity to explore interactions among transformations that may be difficult or tedious for a human engineer to investigate manually.

Leary’s “Optimizin’ Ollie” analogy also made me think about where the advantage of an AI-based stochastic optimizer might actually come from. A human performance engineer usually develops deep expertise in only some parts of the optimization space—for example, one engineer may be particularly strong in loop tiling and fusion, while another may understand instruction scheduling, register pressure, and instruction-level parallelism much more deeply. If there are \(X\) relevant optimization dimensions and an engineer reasons deeply about only a subset of them, even considering combinations of three dimensions gives \(\binom{X}{3}\) possible groups, before accounting for different transformation parameters, orderings, and interactions within each group. This combinatorial growth helps explain Leary’s observation that kernels which human experts already regard as well tuned may still contain significant unrealized performance. What one “Ollie” considers well optimized may still expose opportunities to another specialist with a different set of intuitions. Leary’s proposal seems to be that an AI system can approximate this collective process at much greater breadth: rather than sequentially handing an implementation among many specialists, the model can draw on optimization knowledge spanning many domains, generate new candidate implementations, combine ideas that normally belong to different compiler phases, and repeatedly evaluate those combinations. My initial concern was that some expert optimization decisions arise from intuition that is difficult to express as a clean rule. However, this does not necessarily prevent a search-based system from discovering useful unconventional choices. AlphaGo’s well-known Move 37 is a useful analogy: the move initially appeared highly unusual to expert Go players, yet its value became evident from the resulting play. I would not take this as evidence that an LLM necessarily possesses superior compiler intuition, but it illustrates how search guided by a learned model can explore choices outside conventional human expectations. For a heavily reused GPU kernel, exploring a very large number of such variants may therefore be economically reasonable. The practical question for me is not whether AI can explore a larger optimization space than a single human expert—it clearly can—but whether the additional performance obtained from that larger search space justifies the additional cost of search, correctness verification, and maintaining or regenerating the resulting implementation.

I initially agreed with Leary’s statement that “AI optimization is particularly well suited to mathematical operations which have very strong contracts,” because mathematical workloads seem to offer a cleaner basis for specifying and checking correctness. However, even apparently simple mathematical identities can become complicated once they are mapped onto programming-language and machine semantics. One example I remember from my compiler coursework is reassociation of addition. At the source level, a parser may construct either \((a+b)+c\) or \(a+(b+c)\) depending on the grammar and associativity rules, but treating these two expressions as interchangeable is a separate question of optimization legality. Over mathematical integers, addition is associative, but machine arithmetic introduces additional semantics. For floating-point values in particular, \((a+b)+c\) and \(a+(b+c)\) can produce different results because each intermediate operation is rounded. This made me reconsider how “strong” even a mathematical contract really is in a compiler: the higher-level equation may be simple, but the correctness specification must precisely capture the numerical semantics under which transformations are allowed. My compiler coursework avoided floating-point support, and that now seems significant to me because supporting floating point makes seemingly elementary algebraic optimizations much harder to justify correctly.

I am less convinced by Leary’s suggestion that engineers do not necessarily need to read or understand the generated implementation, even though I understand his analogy to compiler-generated assembly. In ordinary software development, I have observed a growing asymmetry with AI-generated code: producing a large amount of code can be extremely cheap, while reviewing that code for correctness, maintainability, unintended interactions, and future failure modes remains comparatively expensive. If generation becomes much cheaper than verification, maintainers can be overwhelmed by implementations whose authors themselves may not fully understand, potentially reducing the time available for meaningful contributions and threatening the long-term health of a project. I therefore think the success of Leary’s model depends heavily on whether the AI-generated implementation can truly be treated as a disposable compiler artifact, rather than as source code that humans will later need to debug and maintain. If engineers maintain only the high-level specification and can reliably regenerate and independently verify the low-level implementation, then his assembly-code analogy becomes much stronger. However, if a production failure eventually requires a human engineer to inspect, diagnose, or modify the generated implementation, then lack of human comprehensibility becomes an engineering cost rather than merely an aesthetic concern.
